Game design case study

ReSetna

Game Designer · Today's Games · 2023–2025

I was the only game designer on ReSetna, while also being its producer. Every system, enemy, boss, and balancing number went through me. This page covers the design side: the systems I built, how I planned the world, and how I balanced combat.

The design goal was to make the game challenging without making it a souls-like. We opted for no difficulty selection.

The challenge had to come from the game itself, and it had to feel fair. Every system on this page was designed with that in mind: the player should get better by learning the game, not by grinding.

Design work

01

The upgrade chip system

The idea
Most metroidvanias upgrade you with a list of stat boosts. I wanted upgrades to be a puzzle the player solves, and I wanted them to change how abilities work, not only how strong they are.
How it works
Chips are Tetris-shaped pieces made of four blocks. The player rotates them and places them on a grid that starts at 4×4 and can be expanded four times, up to 8×8. There are four chip types, each with its own color. When chips of the same color touch, they unlock higher tiers, so space on the grid becomes the real resource. Loadouts let the player save setups and switch playstyle quickly.

What the chips do
I designed 26 chips. Some unlock new things, like a ranged attack for each weapon, or a dash that damages every enemy you pass through. Others change how an ability behaves: a dodge that leaves an explosive mine behind, axe hits that chain lightning to nearby enemies, or a chip that saves you from a killing blow once. Only a few are plain stat boosts.
Tied to the economy
Chips are bought with Scrap, which enemies drop. In playtests, players were running past enemies because there was no reason to fight them. Linking Scrap to chips gave every fight a reward.

02

Designing the world around abilities

The idea
In a metroidvania, the map is designed around the abilities that unlock it. I planned that on paper before any level was built.
How I did it
The first six zones each give the player one new movement ability: double jump, shield, dash, wall jump, ledge jump and warp. The three weapons, with three versions of each, are spread across the zones. Every zone has the same budget of rewards: 10 chips, plus collectibles like scannable QR codes, corrupted data files and seeds for an NPC's garden. For each zone, I listed which of those rewards sit behind which ability, so every new ability would open something in the zones the player had already visited.

Every zone holds 10 chips and its share of collectibles. Some of them can only be reached with abilities from later zones, which is what pulls the player back.

Why
This made sure that every time the player came back to an old zone with a new ability, there was something there for them. It also gave a clear checklist of what each area needed to hold before anyone started building it.

03

Balancing combat with a hits-to-kill model

The idea
Instead of tuning damage numbers by feel, I balanced combat around one question: how many hits does it take to kill each enemy with each weapon?
How I did it
I put every weapon attack (ground, air, ranged, charged and the downward slam) and every enemy's health and damage into one sheet. The sheet calculated the hits to kill for every combination, so I could see the whole game's balance in one place.

Why
Enemy health goes from 50 for the smallest drones to 1,500 for the largest robot. With the model I could see right away that a drone dies in one spear hit and the big robot takes about 21, and decide if that felt right for each enemy's role in the game.
What playtests changed
Combat responsiveness was our lowest survey score, at 5.5 out of 10. I added animation cancelling and directional attacks, gave enemy parries a clear warning effect, and removed the slow motion on combos so fights keep their momentum.

04

Seven bosses, seven different ideas

The idea
I didn't want seven versions of the same fight. Each boss got its own core idea, and that idea also decided how expensive the boss would be to build.

A pure fight

A very fast, very aggressive duel that tests everything the player has learned up to that point.

Breaking the armor

A heavily armored guard. The player has to break its armor before it takes real damage, while dodging its laser attacks.

Fighting the arena

A room flooded with acid. The boss sends acid waves the player has to jump over, while it attacks with saw blades.

A boss you don't fight

You run from its hallucinations toward a central point where you can shut them down. An expensive boss became a much cheaper chase sequence.

Two bosses, two dimensions

Only one can be hit at a time and the player chooses when to switch. Switching too early or too late gets punished.

A rotating arena

The arena turns while the boss moves energy capsules around it. The player has to turn those capsules against her.

The final boss

A symbolic encounter. The real challenge is the hard climb that leads up to it.

What I took from it

As a producer I saw that some decisions were resource heavy, but as a designer my job was to make the game fun, so I had to find the compromise between the two and design the game around this constraint. It taught me that one system can do several jobs for the player. The chips are a puzzle, an upgrade path and a reason to fight every enemy at the same time.

I learned that upgrades are more interesting when they change how the player plays, not only how strong they are, and that not every big moment needs to be a fight. The bosses that became a chase or a hard climb gave players a change of pace and kept the bosses feeling fresh.

Most of all, I learned that difficulty is about readability. The fixes that made combat feel fair were about letting players cancel their attacks and see what an enemy was about to do, not about lowering numbers. When the player understands why they died, they want to try again.
